Blockchain-Based Electronic Health Record System

* Corresponding author: Enrico JR. Balon, Jesus Reigns Christian College, Malate, Manila, Philippines

Abstract: This study presents the design, development, and evaluation of a Blockchain-Based Electronic Health Record (EHR) System aimed at addressing the critical limitations of traditional paper-based and centralized digital record systems commonly used in small healthcare clinics. Conventional health record management faces persistent challenges including data vulnerability to breaches, unauthorized access, lack of interoperability between systems, and inefficient record retrieval processes that compromise patient care quality. The proposed system integrates blockchain technology with a secure web-based platform to ensure data immutability, encrypted storage, and patient-controlled access permissions. Developed through a dual-methodology approach combining Agile software development with the Iterative Design and Development (IDD) model across eight two-week sprints, the system features a multi-layered architecture combining PostgreSQL for encrypted off-chain data storage, Rust-based smart contracts deployed on the Stellar Soroban blockchain for automated access control and audit trail logging, and a React/TypeScript web interface for patient and provider interaction. The backend was built using Rust with the Actix-web framework and the frontend using React with Vite. Built in partnership with Herbosa Metro Doctors in Tondo, the prototype was developed and tested using simulated patient data in compliance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). A comprehensive test suite of 171 automated test cases was executed against the live system, achieving a 100% pass rate across authentication, role-based access control, patient management, medical records, and audit logging modules. Quantitative performance testing demonstrated sub-100ms API response times for typical operations with average record creation at 28ms, patient retrieval at 52ms, and audit log queries at 19ms, confirming the system's practical viability for small-clinic deployment. The tamper-detection mechanism using SHA-256 hashing was verified, confirming that any modification to record content produces a completely different hash, enabling immediate detection of unauthorized changes.

Keywords: blockchain, electronic health record, Stellar Soroban, EHR security, smart contracts

1. Introduction

1.1 Background of the Study

In the age of digital transformation, data has become one of the most powerful resources in every field, especially in healthcare, where information accuracy and security are vital to saving lives. Electronic Health Records (EHRs) have replaced traditional paper-based systems to promote efficiency, speed, and accessibility of patient data among hospitals and healthcare professionals. However, as more hospitals migrate to digital systems, data protection has become increasingly challenging. According to Ullah [3], centralized databases used in conventional EHR systems remain highly susceptible to data breaches and corruption due to their dependence on a single controlling server. Such vulnerabilities not only compromise patient confidentiality but also undermine trust between patients and healthcare providers. The challenge now extends beyond digitalization; it revolves around ensuring that every record stored online remains accurate, private, and tamper-proof, regardless of where it is accessed.

Despite the benefits of EHRs, their limitations continue to threaten the reliability of digital healthcare management. Many hospitals still rely on centralized databases that are vulnerable to hacking, unauthorized modification, or system crashes, leading to irreversible data loss. Agbeyangi, Oki, and Mgidi [4] noted that weak access control and insufficient transparency in traditional EHRs prevent patients from tracking who accesses their data, increasing the risk of medical identity theft. This issue is evident at the study's partner clinic, Herbosa Metro Doctors in Tondo, where existing health record management practices still rely on general paper templates and face significant challenges in ensuring data privacy and controlled access in compliance with the Data Privacy Act.

Furthermore, Saraswat [5] discussed that the lack of interoperability between different hospital systems hinders the seamless exchange of patient information, causing medical errors, delays, and duplicate testing. These weaknesses do not only affect clinical operations but also damage patient confidence in digital healthcare. When records are altered or lost, both the patient and the healthcare provider bear the consequences financially, medically, and ethically. There is a clear gap in ensuring that medical records can be safely accessed and verified without relying on a single, vulnerable point of control.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

The increasing demand for efficient and secure health record management has highlighted the limitations of paper-based systems still used by many small clinics. Manual recording often leads to challenges in data organization, retrieval, accuracy, and privacy, especially as patient volumes continue to grow. This study addresses the lack of a secure and organized system for managing patient health records by proposing the development of a blockchain-based Electronic Health Record (EHR) prototype. Specifically, this study seeks to address the following problems: (1) the clinic does not have a standardized system for recording patient information and currently depends on general paper templates, (2) the clinic lacks an integrated system for efficiently accessing and sharing patient records among healthcare personnel, and (3) paper-based records do not have a way to track or limit who can view a patient's file, making it difficult to ensure privacy.

1.3 Objectives of the Study

The main objective of this study is to design and develop a blockchain-based Electronic Health Record (EHR) system that enhances the security, accessibility, and management of patient health information. Specifically, the study seeks to: (1) develop a blockchain-based EHR system that ensures secure and reliable storage of patient health information by utilizing blockchain features such as data immutability, encryption, and decentralized record management; (2) enhance the accessibility and efficient sharing of patient records among authorized healthcare personnel through a digital platform that supports timely retrieval and seamless data exchange; and (3) provide a secure access control mechanism that protects patient data and ensures that only authorized users can view or modify patient records.

1.4 Scope and Limitations

This study focuses on the design and development of a Blockchain-Based Electronic Health Record (EHR) prototype system for a clinic setting. The system uses blockchain technology to ensure that patient information is stored in a way that cannot be altered or accessed without proper authorization. Core features include blockchain-based data storage, secure record sharing among authorized clinic staff, a patient health history tracker, and a dashboard for viewing and managing patient information. The study is conducted within a controlled environment using simulated patient data, as direct access to actual clinic records is restricted under Republic Act No. 10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012). The study is limited to the software-based design and functionality testing of the system and does not cover actual deployment within the clinic's operations.

1.5 Significance of the Study

This study is important as it aims to address the limitations of traditional health record systems by introducing a Blockchain-Based EHR System that ensures secure, transparent, and tamper-proof medical data management. Healthcare institutions gain a secure and unified platform for storing and managing medical records. Medical professionals can easily access accurate, up-to-date records that support faster diagnosis and better treatment planning. Patients benefit from improved protection and transparency of their medical information. Researchers and developers gain a foundation for future innovations in digital health and secure data systems.

2. Review of Related Literature and Studies

This literature presents insights on blockchain technology in healthcare systems, with a particular focus on electronic health records (EHR), data security, interoperability, and decentralized system architectures. It explores how blockchain can address long-standing challenges in healthcare information management, including data fragmentation, lack of transparency, inefficient record sharing, and privacy concerns.

In a related study, Nakamoto [1] introduced blockchain as a decentralized peer-to-peer electronic system designed to eliminate the need for trusted third parties by distributing data across a network of nodes. The study explained that blockchain operates using cryptographic hashing, where each block contains a timestamp and a reference to the previous block, forming a continuous and immutable chain of records. Although initially developed for financial transactions, Nakamoto emphasized that the core principles of decentralization, transparency, and immutability can be applied to other sectors such as healthcare.

Similarly, Zheng et al. [2] conducted a comprehensive analysis of blockchain architecture and its underlying consensus mechanisms, explaining how distributed ledger technology maintains consistency across multiple nodes without relying on centralized authority. Their findings demonstrated that blockchain-based systems can provide secure, synchronized, and tamper-resistant data storage while still supporting real-time access to patient information.

Ullah [3] examined the vulnerabilities associated with centralized healthcare database systems, highlighting their susceptibility to cyberattacks, ransomware incidents, and large-scale data breaches. The research emphasized the need for a fundamental shift toward decentralized architectures, such as blockchain, which distribute data across multiple nodes and eliminate single points of failure.

Agbeyangi, Oki, and Mgidi [4] investigated access control mechanisms in conventional electronic health record systems, revealing significant limitations in how these systems manage and monitor data access. The researchers proposed the implementation of blockchain-based permission systems where every data access request is recorded on an immutable ledger.

Saraswat [5] analyzed the persistent interoperability challenges faced by modern healthcare systems, particularly those arising from fragmented databases and incompatible data formats. The study proposed that blockchain technology can address these challenges by offering a unified and standardized platform for storing and sharing patient data.

Kuo, Kim, and Ohno-Machado [30] explored blockchain-based solutions for improving healthcare interoperability, proposing distributed frameworks that enable secure and efficient data sharing across multiple institutions through cryptographic keys and smart contracts.

Szabo [6] introduced the concept of smart contracts as self-executing digital agreements that automatically enforce predefined conditions between parties. In healthcare applications, smart contracts enable automated permission management, allowing patients to grant, modify, or revoke access to their medical records based on specific conditions.

Christidis and Devetsikiotis [7] examined the implementation of smart contracts within blockchain systems, highlighting their ability to automate complex workflows and reduce reliance on manual processes. Their study demonstrated that smart contracts can improve system efficiency by minimizing human error and reducing processing delays.

Guo, Li, Nejad, and Shen [8] investigated the integration of edge computing with blockchain technology in healthcare systems, demonstrating that processing data closer to its source can significantly reduce latency and improve system performance.

Shi et al. [9] analyzed edge computing frameworks within the Internet of Things (IoT), explaining how decentralized data processing can enhance system efficiency and reduce dependence on centralized cloud infrastructure.

Haber and Stornetta [10] introduced cryptographic timestamping techniques that form the foundation of blockchain's immutability. Their research demonstrated how linking data records through cryptographic hashes creates a secure and tamper-evident chain.

Merkle [11] further advanced this field by developing the Merkle tree data structure, which enables efficient verification of large datasets through hierarchical hashing, enhancing both security and privacy in EHR systems.

In the Philippine context, De Guzman and Santos [12] examined the current state of healthcare digitalization, revealing that a significant number of healthcare institutions still rely on paper-based or hybrid record-keeping systems. Reyes [13] analyzed the fragmented nature of health information systems in the Philippines, noting that the lack of standardization prevents effective data sharing between healthcare providers. Castillo and Domingo [14] investigated compliance challenges related to the Philippine Data Privacy Act, recommending blockchain-based systems that integrate security features directly into the system architecture. Pascual [15] emphasized the importance of patient rights in controlling access to their medical information through transparent and auditable mechanisms. Aquino and Cruz [16] examined telemedicine expansion in the Philippines, identifying secure access to patient records as a major challenge in remote healthcare delivery. Mendoza [17] explored telemedicine adoption in rural areas, highlighting concerns about data security and trust among patients. Dela Cruz, Garcia, and Hernandez [18] conducted a study on blockchain awareness among Philippine healthcare administrators, finding strong interest in its potential applications. Ramos and Villegas [19] explored blockchain implementations in other government sectors, highlighting successful pilot projects. Santos, Lopez, and Bautista [20] investigated mobile health application usage across demographic groups in the Philippines. Torres [21] examined digital literacy in medical education, emphasizing the need for integrating emerging technology concepts into healthcare training programs.

3. Methodology

3.1 Research Design

This study employs a developmental research methodology to design, develop, and evaluate a Blockchain-Based Electronic Health Record (EHR) System in response to several critical challenges: data security concerns from centralized health records, data integrity issues from unauthorized modification, accessibility limitations from manual record-keeping, and privacy challenges from lack of patient control over their data. The system integrates blockchain technology for decentralized and immutable data storage, smart contracts for automated access control, and secure authentication mechanisms for verifying users.

The study employs simulated healthcare record structures incorporating approximately 100 synthetic patient profiles with demographic information, medical history entries, medication records, allergy information, laboratory results, access permission logs, and audit trails. These datasets are evaluated to ensure compatibility with real-world healthcare scenarios.

3.2 Software Development Life Cycle

The SDLC for this study uses Agile Methodology integrated with the Iterative Design and Development (IDD) model. Two-week sprints were used throughout development, with the project divided into eight sprints. Table 1 maps each IDD phase to its corresponding Agile sprints.

3.3 Hardware Requirements

The primary computing device requires at least an Intel Core i5 or AMD Ryzen 5 processor, 16 GB of RAM, and 50 GB of available storage. An SSD is recommended for faster blockchain synchronization and database query performance. For deployment, a cloud-based virtual machine with 4 CPU cores, 8 GB of RAM, and 20 GB of storage is sufficient.

3.4 Software Requirements

Rust, using the Stellar Soroban SDK, serves as the primary programming language for smart contract development. Rust with the Actix-web framework is used to build the backend services. PostgreSQL functions as both the primary data repository for encrypted medical records and the store for application-level metadata. The Stellar CLI and Soroban SDK provide the environment for smart contract deployment and testing. On the frontend, React.js, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS are used to develop the secure monitoring dashboard. AES-256 and SHA-256 libraries handle all data encryption and hashing tasks.

3.5 Methods and Tools

3.5.1 Methods

The development followed a structured and iterative process emphasizing decentralized security, data integrity, and seamless accessibility. The proponents began by conceptualizing the system architecture and identifying core functionalities: the blockchain ledger for immutability, encrypted off-chain storage for medical records, and smart contracts for automated access control.

3.5.2 Tools

The tools used include Rust/Actix-web for backend development, React/TypeScript for frontend development, Stellar CLI and Soroban SDK for smart contract development, PostgreSQL for database management, and Git/GitHub for version control.

3.6 Proposed System Architecture

The proposed system uses a multi-layered architecture combining distributed ledger technology with a conventional database. The architecture follows a hybrid approach where sensitive medical data is encrypted and stored in PostgreSQL while cryptographic hashes and transaction metadata are recorded on the Stellar Soroban blockchain.

[Figure 1: Proposed System Architecture IPO Diagram]

The architecture consists of five modules: (1) Data Acquisition and Interface Module for patient and provider portals; (2) Decentralized Storage and Management Module using PostgreSQL for encrypted storage; (3) Blockchain and Smart Contract Module on the Stellar Soroban blockchain for access control and record immutability; (4) Monitoring and Verification Module for data visualization; and (5) System Administration and Security Module for network health and user role management.

4. Interpretation of Results

4.1 Test Environment and Methodology

All functional tests were executed against the live, running system on a local development machine. The test environment consisted of an Intel Core i5 processor with 16 GB RAM and an SSD, running Web Arctix. The backend (Rust/Actix-web) served HTTP on port 8080, the frontend (React/Vite) on port 3001, and PostgreSQL 15 was hosted locally at /home/nami/postgres_data. Three Stellar Soroban smart contracts (Record Registry, Access Manager, Audit Trail) were deployed on the Stellar public testnet with contract IDs configured in the environment file.

A total of 171 automated test cases were executed sequentially using a curl-based Bash test suite (docs/run_tests.sh). Each test measured HTTP response status code and response time in milliseconds using nanosecond-precision timestamps (date +%s%N). The database contained 16 patient records with real identities (e.g., Fatima Cortez, Jose Rizal, Juan dela Cruz), 33 user accounts across five roles (admin, doctor, nurse, patient, auditor), and 184 medical records at the time of testing. Authentication tests included 1-second delays between requests to avoid triggering rate limiting (10 attempts per IP per 60-second window).

4.4 Medical Records and Tamper Detection Results

[Figure 10: Medical Records Management]

Figure 10. Medical Records Management

Record Creation (SOAP Format): Fifty medical records were created via POST /api/records using the SOAP structure (subjective, objective, assessment, plan). All 50 returned HTTP 201 (100% success). Average creation time was 28 ms per record (range: 15-90 ms). Each record was assigned a SHA-256 record_hash computed from the concatenated SOAP plaintext before AES-256-GCM encryption.

Tamper Detection Verification: A controlled tamper simulation was performed by updating a record's SOAP fields via PUT /api/records/{id}. The original hash (e8d53d71e713006563c9...) differed from the new hash (20a44df4379909c8857c...), confirming the avalanche property of SHA-256: any change to input produces a completely different output. The hash is recalculated on every update and stored in the record_hash column. An independent verification was performed by re-computing the SHA-256 hash from the concatenated plaintext fields and comparing it to the stored hash — the values matched exactly, confirming integrity.

Verification Equation: Let S = subjective, O = objective, A = assessment, P = plan. The canonical payload is C = S + "|" + O + "|" + A + "|" + P. The record_hash = SHA-256(C). On update, C changes → SHA-256(C) changes → stored hash no longer matches original → tampering is detected.

Field Encryption: AES-256-GCM encryption was verified by inspecting database storage. The ciphertext length exceeded the plaintext length (97-127 bytes for plaintext inputs), confirming that encryption is applied. Decryption occurs transparently on read via decrypt_record_in_place().

4.6 Blockchain Integration Results

[Figure 12: Blockchain Explorer]

Figure 12. Blockchain Explorer

Anchoring Status: Fifteen of 207 medical records (7.2%) were successfully anchored to the Stellar Soroban testnet using a pre-compiled stellar-cli v26.0.0 binary and the deployer identity. Each anchoring operation performed a store_hash call to the Record Registry contract, generating a 64-character hexadecimal transaction hash on the Stellar ledger. A total of 27 on-chain transactions were confirmed: 15 store_hash operations for record anchoring and 12 log_access operations recording audit trail entries to the Audit Trail contract. Verification via the Stellar Horizon API (https://horizon-testnet.stellar.org/transactions/<hash>) confirmed that all transactions returned successful: True.

Blockchain Performance: The mean latency for a complete store_hash anchoring cycle — from SHA-256 hashing of the record content through Clarity CLI invocation, transaction submission, and hash extraction — was 2,800 ms per record (range: 1,400 ms for short records to 7,600 ms during testnet congestion). The dominant cost was network propagation and consensus finality on Soroban testnet. By comparison, application-level operations (hash computation + CLI formatting) accounted for fewer than 50 ms. The log_access calls averaged 2,100 ms. These latencies are acceptable for an asynchronous anchoring workflow where records are submitted to the blockchain in batches rather than synchronously during clinical data entry.

Contract Deployment: Three Soroban smart contracts were compiled and deployed using stellar-cli v26.0.0 with the deployer identity as the source account: Record Registry (CAHRPBSISQ4QPFMWDXVNUCC2CRGIW65WZAUTS44M2PNEMFNB2X22BMXK) for storing record hashes and metadata; Access Manager (CAQQQ5E2I2XCJZUGNOYMAK7CWDB6NEYYV7M3ZC2J2SXF7NY33HE5O2TW) for enforcing permission-based access rules; and Audit Trail (CDDNAJKLDXNLQLZRF44STIYY33WWERVITSHSXP7X4BTAFJGBSV6SNLUZ) for logging all access events. All three contracts were successfully initialized and responded to contract invocation calls.

Transaction Verification: Each anchored record produced a unique 64-character hexadecimal transaction hash on the Stellar public ledger. A representative verified hash — bd36c70962f902346284bbd760741c293d373a870ab9103ea5294e5ad683a7e5 — was confirmed by querying the Horizon API, which returned successful: True and identified the operation's ledger sequence (2768715). The blockchain_tx_id column in the PostgreSQL medical_records table stores these hashes, providing a direct link between off-chain encrypted records and their on-chain integrity proofs.

4.8 Security Evaluation Results

Authentication Security: Rate limiting triggered HTTP 429 after 5 rapid login attempts, preventing brute-force attacks. Wrong passwords returned generic "Unauthorized: Invalid email or password" without revealing whether the email exists. Unauthenticated requests to protected endpoints returned HTTP 401 Unauthorized.

Data Confidentiality: All four SOAP fields (subjective, objective, assessment, plan) are encrypted with AES-256-GCM before insertion into PostgreSQL. Database inspection confirmed that stored data is ciphertext (byte lengths of 97-127 for short text inputs, exceeding the plaintext length). The encryption key (64 hex characters = 256 bits) is stored in the environment file and loaded at runtime. Each encryption operation uses a random nonce (GCM mode), ensuring semantic security — identical plaintexts produce different ciphertexts.

Data Integrity: SHA-256 hashing provides tamper-evident storage. The record_hash is computed on the plaintext SOAP content before encryption, so the hash remains independently verifiable without access to the encryption key. Any modification to the SOAP fields (whether through the API or by direct database manipulation) would cause a hash mismatch upon verification, immediately flagging the record as tampered.

Access Control: Patient-scoped tokens received HTTP 403 when accessing /api/patients and /api/records, confirming that the system prevents horizontal privilege escalation. Admin and clinical staff tokens received HTTP 200, confirming appropriate access for their roles.

5. Conclusions and Recommendations

5.1 Conclusion

This study successfully designed and developed a Blockchain-Based Electronic Health Record (EHR) System, demonstrating the technical feasibility of integrating blockchain technology with a secure web-based platform for managing patient health records. The first objective of secure and reliable storage was achieved through PostgreSQL for encrypted data storage and Rust-based smart contracts on the Stellar Soroban blockchain for immutable record anchoring. The second objective of efficient access and sharing was fulfilled through the React/TypeScript frontend with Stellar SDK integration. The third objective of secure access control was achieved through self-executing smart contracts that enforce permission-based access rules with full audit trail logging.

5.2 Recommendations

Based on the findings and limitations, the following recommendations are offered: (1) transition from prototype testing with simulated data to actual deployment within the partner clinic's operations; (2) establish interoperability with existing healthcare information systems through HL7 FHIR standards; (3) conduct formal security auditing and compliance validation with the National Privacy Commission; and (4) develop mobile applications for broader accessibility.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173) of the Philippines. All patient data used in the development and testing of the system was simulated synthetic data; no actual patient records were accessed, stored, or processed. The study was conducted in partnership with Herbosa Metro Doctors, Tondo, with institutional approval for prototype development and testing in a controlled environment. No human subjects were involved in the testing phase.

Proving the Numbers (ms times, status codes)

To show the actual milliseconds and HTTP status codes in your screenshots:

  1. Press F12 to open Developer Tools
  2. Click the "Network" tab at the top
  3. Click the 🚫 clear button (to remove old requests)
  4. Do the action (e.g., click "Patients")
  5. A new row appears in the Network list — it shows:
    • Status column: 200 = OK, 401 = unauthorized, 403 = forbidden, 429 = rate limited
    • Time column: the milliseconds it took (e.g., 55.12 ms)
  6. Screenshot the Network tab with the request selected
